Built by MIT and Stanford engineers to make structured-data AI safe

An architecture decades in the making, now running enterprise AI in production at Fortune 500 companies on hardware small enough to hold in one hand.

Decades in the making

Fractal Computing's architecture was designed in the early 1980s by MIT and Stanford computer scientists for a government agency project. The core innovation, Locality Optimization, was built to solve a fundamental problem: data and compute were too far apart, and the gap was making applications slow and expensive.

The science was ahead of the hardware. It took four decades for commodity processors to catch up. When they did, Fractal's architecture made possible enterprise-scale AI applications that are safe, fast and cheap to run.

Today the platform runs on off-the-shelf hardware. Fortune 500 companies in utilities, telecommunications and financial services run production AI workloads on Fractal digital twins, including billing, customer care, rate planning and transaction processing, serving tens of millions of customers with zero risk of data corruption in their systems of record.

Timeline

  1. 1980s

    Research begins with a team of MIT and Stanford computer scientists working on a government agency project. Locality Optimization is developed as the core architectural principle.

  2. 2000s

    Commodity hardware catches up as off-the-shelf processors reach the performance needed to run the architecture at enterprise scale.

  3. 2010s

    First Fortune 500 deployments go live in utilities and telecommunications, running applications for millions of customers on hardware that fits on a desk.

  4. Today

    Enterprise AI in production across utilities, telecommunications and financial services, with Fractal as the distributed computing and storage layer for Enterprise AI applications.
